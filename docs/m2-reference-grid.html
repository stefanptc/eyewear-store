A side-by-side read of six premium eyewear sites, scored on the five axes that decide what our store looks like. Use this to commit a direction — type, colour, hero, card, motion — before any pixel of the actual store gets pushed.

Each site is grouped by a one-line characterisation, then broken into five dimensions. After all six, the synthesis section turns the comparison into three explicit decisions to make. Mark your picks at the bottom.

01

Warby Parker

warbyparker.com →

The North Star of D2C eyewear. Built the playbook everyone else copies: home try-on, transparent pricing, lifestyle photography. Premium-accessible — not luxury, not cheap.

Type
Sans-serif, geometric, modest weight contrast. Proxima Nova-family. Hierarchy by size, not weight — most type at one or two weights. Reads as quiet confidence. No serifs, no decorative type.
Colour
White base, warm neutrals, occasional saturated category colour. Cream, soft pink, deep navy used as section dividers. No single ownable accent. Seasonal campaigns shift the palette without breaking the system.
white / warm neutrals / navy
Hero
Lifestyle photography led. Models wearing frames, often editorial portraits, occasional product hero for new launches. Frequently rotates by campaign. Faces sell trust in this category.
Card
Clean grid, 4-up desktop, 2-up mobile. Frame on flat ground, name + price below, colour swatches as dots, "Staff pick" stickers. Card itself is unframed — image bleeds to whitespace. Quick-view + try-on add directly from grid.
Motion
Restrained. Subtle scroll reveals, smooth nav transitions, no parallax theatre. Interactivity is functional (the famous face that follows your cursor on PDPs), not decorative.
Friendly premium · accessible · trust-by-warmth
02

Ace & Tate

aceandtate.com →

The European answer to Warby. Amsterdam-headquartered, slightly more design-forward, more willing to use bold colour blocking and editorial typography. Closest reference to where you should land tonally.

Type
Modern geometric sans, sharper than Warby. Custom sans for display, paired with a refined body sans. Editorial use of all-caps section labels. Confident weight contrast on collection pages.
Colour
Off-white base, collection-driven colour blocking. Often pairs a saturated background (mustard, terracotta, deep teal) with a neutral product shot. Palette shifts seasonally — same design system, different colours.
off-white + rotating accents
Hero
Editorial split between lifestyle and product-as-still-life. Big full-bleed image, type overlaid bottom-left. Frequently shoots frames on coloured surfaces (almost still-life painterly). More art direction than Warby.
Card
Generous, 3-up desktop, image-dominant. Frame floats over coloured background that often matches the collection. Colour name (e.g., "Inkblot") sits next to colour dot. Hover swaps to lifestyle shot.
Motion
Moderate, considered. Hover state on cards swaps imagery. Smooth carousels on collection pages. Page transitions feel "designed." Doesn't tip into theatrics.
European playful premium · trust-by-craft
03

Cubitts

cubitts.com →

King's Cross London, hand-built frames, museum-grade product photography. The most editorial of the six. Reads like a craft journal more than a shop — long-form provenance copy, archive-feeling type.

Type
Serif-led, distinctly British. Old-style serif for display (Caslon-feel), refined sans for UI/body. Heavy use of italic for emphasis and section labels. Type is the design — does most of the heavy lifting on premium signal.
Colour
Cream, ink, one warm accent. Restraint as identity. The whole site lives in maybe four colours. No campaign colour shifts. Palette is the brand.
cream / ink / brass
Hero
Product as object. Frames photographed like museum pieces — on plinths, against neutral seamless, sometimes with provenance objects (acetate blanks, hand tools). Type-led headlines. Rarely a face.
Card
Roomy, low-density, archival. Frame on cream, name in serif italic, model number in mono, price understated. The card feels like a museum label, not a product tile.
Motion
Almost none. Type stays still. Images don't ken-burns. Scroll is fast. The restraint is part of the premium signal — anything moving would feel cheap here.
Heritage british craftsmanship · trust-by-archive
04

MOSCOT

moscot.com →

NYC since 1915. Four generations. Yellow is the brand. Heritage signalling without irony — uses its century of history as the primary trust device. Most "brand-led" of the six.

Type
Heritage serif display + clean sans body. Strong New York signage influence. The "M" wordmark is iconic. Display type often set tight and large; body type stays utilitarian.
Colour
Black, white, MOSCOT yellow. The yellow (#fdc82f-ish) is ownable and unmistakable. No other accent gets close. Everything else is grayscale.
ink / white / signature yellow
Hero
Editorial split — bold image, bold type. Magazine-cover energy. Often portraits of cultural figures wearing the frames (musicians, actors). Sometimes archival B&W to lean into the heritage.
Card
Tight grid, frame-name-as-character. Each frame has a Yiddish or Lower East Side name (Lemtosh, Zev, Yenta) treated typographically. Card feels like a catalogue page from an old optician.
Motion
Moderate, theatrical at hero, calm elsewhere. Hero often has bold reveals or video. Below the fold it stays out of the way.
NYC heritage · trust-by-history
05

Bailey Nelson

baileynelson.com.au →

Australian D2C, family-feeling. Same affordability angle as Warby but warmer in tone, sunnier in art direction. Closer to what an "accessible Romanian premium" might feel like if it leaned warm.

Type
Friendly sans-serif, slightly humanist. Less geometric than Warby, more warmth in the letterforms. Pairs with occasional script or handwritten flourishes in editorial moments.
Colour
Warm cream base, soft sage, terracotta accents. Reads as "californian / coastal." Multiple warm neutrals, no single ownable accent. Sunshine in pixel form.
cream / sage / terracotta
Hero
Lifestyle photography, outdoor and casual. Often models laughing, near water, in natural light. Less editorial polish than A&T, more "real people in real moments."
Card
3-up, warm-toned backgrounds. Frame on cream or sage. Name + price + colour dot. Often pairs the product with a small lifestyle inset on hover.
Motion
Smooth, modern, friendly. Soft fades, gentle reveals, nothing aggressive. The motion language matches the colour language — warm, slow, unhurried.
Australian casual premium · trust-by-warmth
06

Dresden Vision

dresden.vision →

Their pitch: modular frames where lenses and arms are interchangeable. The visual system mirrors the product — Swiss, functional, near-IKEA. The most aesthetically opinionated of the six.

Type
Utilitarian sans, near-Helvetica. Strict hierarchy, modest sizes, no serifs anywhere. Type does its job and gets out of the way — product is the hero.
Colour
White + bold primary colour blocks. Each frame colour gets its own swatch hero — green, red, blue, yellow. The colour is the product, so the site treats colour as the index.
white / ink / many product colours
Hero
Product-led, Swiss design. Frames photographed flat, often exploded-view to show the modular system. Faces appear, but the product configuration is what's selling.
Card
Modular, configurable, almost shopping a system. Cards emphasise interchangeability — pick frame colour, pick arms, pick lens. Closer to a furniture configurator than a glasses page.
Motion
Minimal, functional. Configurator moves; everything else doesn't. Premium-via-restraint, like Cubitts, but cooler and more industrial.
Utilitarian swiss · trust-by-system

Three decisions,
before any pixel.

Every later choice flows from these three. Pick one option per axis — gut, not committee. We can reverse later, but the cost compounds.

Axis 01

Typography direction

A
Serif-led editorial Heritage trust signal. The serif does the premium work. Best when product is the hero and the brand wants to feel established.

→ Cubitts, MOSCOT

B
Modern sans-only Clean, accessible, contemporary. Premium-via-restraint. Lighter cognitive load. Closest to the Warby playbook.

→ Warby Parker, Bailey Nelson, Dresden

C
Mixed — serif display + sans body Best of both. Serif headlines carry heritage / trust, sans body stays modern and readable. The most flexible direction, the hardest to execute (typeface pairing has to land).

→ Ace & Tate

Axis 02

Colour direction

A
Monochrome + one ownable accent Disciplined. Black/cream + a single signature colour the brand owns. Strongest brand recognition. Hardest to keep interesting across many pages.

→ MOSCOT (yellow), Cubitts (brass)

B
Warm neutral palette, no single accent Cream, sage, terracotta, navy as section dividers. Forgiving — easy to extend to new collections. Slightly less ownable as identity.

→ Warby, Bailey Nelson

C
Collection-driven colour blocking Off-white base, palette shifts per collection. Most editorial. Demands ongoing art direction (someone curating each new launch).

→ Ace & Tate

D
Product-colour-as-index The frame colours are the palette. Best when the product range is itself colourful. Probably wrong for affordable Rx where most frames are black/tortoise/clear.

→ Dresden Vision

Axis 03

Hero treatment

A
Lifestyle photography led Models wearing frames. Sells trust by showing the product on real faces. Demands real photography — Flux/AI can do moods, not catalogue.

→ Warby Parker, Bailey Nelson, A&T

B
Product as object Frames photographed like museum pieces. Editorial restraint. Works if product photography is exceptional. Cold-feeling if executed badly.

→ Cubitts

C
Editorial split — bold image + bold type Magazine-cover energy. Photo on left, type on right (or overlaid). Hardest to keep fresh — every campaign needs new art direction.

→ MOSCOT, A&T variants

D
Type-led / Swiss Typography is the hero. Minimal imagery, big confident statements. Easiest to execute solo. Risks feeling cold for a face-product category.

→ Dresden, Cubitts variants

My read — not your decision, just my prior

For an affordable Romanian Rx eyewear store where trust is the primary conversion lever and there's no shot budget for endless campaigns: C / B / A — mixed serif+sans, warm neutral palette, lifestyle-led hero.

Why: the serif buys you heritage signal cheaply (Cubitts-style trust). The warm palette is forgiving across many products without per-collection art direction. Lifestyle leads because Romanian customers buying prescription glasses online need to see them on a face before they hand over a card. Closest single reference: Ace & Tate.

Push back if your gut says otherwise. You're the harder critic.
